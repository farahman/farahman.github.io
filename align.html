Why This Matters

Introduction

Introduction: As generative AI becomes increasingly embedded in computing practice, undergraduate computing and engineering education must teach students not only how to use AI, but how to use it appropriately, critically, and responsibly. In this context, graduate and undergraduate teaching assistants are especially important because they shape students’ day-to-day practices in labs, office hours, and peer-support settings, where norms around debugging, problem solving, design reasoning, and academic integrity are often formed. Recent work suggests that students already experience tension in their use of AI: while many value these tools for explanation, feedback, and efficiency, they also question whether AI may reduce genuine engagement with learning. For this reason, TA training on responsible AI use is an important pedagogical intervention rather than an optional add-on. Well-prepared TAs can help students use AI to support learning—for example, by generating hints, comparing approaches, or suggesting test cases—while reinforcing the need to verify outputs, recognize limitations, and justify their own reasoning. Our prior work in large computing courses similarly suggests that AI can support debugging and code review productively when it is framed as a tool for reflection and inquiry, not as a shortcut to solutions.

To respond to this need, we introduce ALIGN (AI Literacy, Instructional Guidance, and Norms), a practical framework for preparing graduate and undergraduate teaching assistants to engage generative AI in ways that are pedagogically sound, critically informed, and aligned with disciplinary learning goals in undergraduate computing and engineering education. Rather than treating AI as either a shortcut to prohibit or a tool to adopt uncritically, ALIGN positions it as a support for inquiry, explanation, verification, and reflection while preserving productive struggle, human judgment, and academic integrity. Institutions can use ALIGN as a scalable roadmap for TA development, faculty-TA alignment, and continuous improvement by combining short training modules, authentic instructional scenarios, shared guidance on acceptable use, and lightweight evidence collection. In this way, the framework helps departments move toward a more critical, consistent, and student-centered model of AI-supported education.

Such training is also important for workforce preparation. As future software engineers and scientists enter professional settings where AI is increasingly integrated into everyday practice, they must learn habits of verification, critical evaluation, and responsible use. Training TAs to model these practices helps strengthen student learning in the present while cultivating the professional judgment needed for an AI-shaped future.

Training Goals

What TAs learn to do

Frame AI as a reasoning aid

Use GenAI to prompt explanation, debugging hypotheses, comparison of alternatives, and design trade-off reflection — not as an answer engine.

Preserve productive struggle

Students articulate the problem, predict causes, write test cases, or explain their own code before consulting any AI output.

Teach verification explicitly

Challenge hallucinations, incomplete explanations, and polished-but-incorrect solutions, modeling how disciplinary judgment operates in real time.

Center contextual appropriateness

The same tool may be legitimate for documentation review or conceptual explanation, but inappropriate when it bypasses the core intellectual work of a task.

Align with inclusive teaching

Address confidence-building feedback and welcoming learning climates. Recognize how AI can either widen or reduce equity depending on how it is introduced.

Instructional Model

What the model looks like in practice

The framework maps key student support needs to concrete TA responses that center learning rather than shortcutting it.

Support context → TA instructional response
Debugging support
TAs ask students to describe the bug first, then use AI to surface possible causes and evaluate which explanation best fits the code and the error trace.
Code explanation
Students compare their own understanding with AI-generated explanations, then critique what is accurate, missing, or misleading.
Documentation & clarity
AI is used to compare alternative documentation or pseudocode so students can revise for completeness, readability, and precision.
Testing & edge cases
Students write their own tests before asking AI for additional scenarios, using the tool to deepen — not outsource — test-driven thinking.
Reflection & transfer
After the interaction, TAs prompt students to explain what they learned, when they would trust or reject the AI output, and how they would solve a similar problem without the tool.
The ALIGN Framework

Four-component training roadmap

Step 01
Modular GenAI Training
Short self-paced modules on appropriate use cases, prompting practices, verification strategies, and reflection prompts tied to course learning goals.
Step 02
Scenario-Based Learning
Authentic instructional scenarios from debugging, design comparison, testing, and code explanation contexts — disciplinary, not generic.
Step 03
Faculty-TA Alignment
Pair TA training with faculty guidance so instructors and assistants send consistent messages about acceptable use, disclosure, and prohibited contexts.
Step 04
Continuous Improvement
Collect lightweight evidence — TA reflections, student feedback, office-hour observations, and error patterns — to improve the model each term.

Broader Impact

Why this framework matters for the community

Provides a concrete roadmap for institutions that need AI guidance for TAs now, especially in high-enrollment computing courses where help-session practices are often inconsistent.

Moves beyond binary "allow vs. ban" policies by identifying trainable behaviors: prompting, verification, reflection, documentation critique, and explanation-centered support.

Offers a scalable faculty-development asset. Self-paced modules prepare new TAs quickly while maintaining shared expectations across multiple sections and semesters.

Models how AI policy can be operationalized at the instructional-support level, where students receive the most informal and consequential guidance about legitimate help.

Contributes to a more inclusive learning environment by preparing TAs to use AI as a scaffolding tool while avoiding dependence, intimidation, or inequitable access to support.
